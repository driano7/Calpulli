# Day of the Dead: when the city lights up its memory

From October 31 to November 2, Mexico City changes the subject. Here death
does not disguise itself: it is visited. You go to the cemetery, light a
candle, eat pan de muerto and remember someone by name. UNESCO recognised it
as intangible cultural heritage in 2008.

## Where

- **Mixcoac Cemetery** (Tlalpan). The largest in Latin America, and the one
  with the tradition of the light. Several entrances: ask for the area with
  the lit graves.
- **San Fernando Cemetery** (Tacubaya). The most famous: Villavicencio,
  Morelos, Altamirano.
- **Alhóndiga de Toluca and San Andrés Cemetery** (Coyoacán). Smaller and
  more intimate, with the green of the neighbourhood around it.
- **La Merced** (Carmen and Moneda streets). Where the sugar skulls, the
  papel picado and the candles are bought.
- **Casa de los Azulejos** (Carranza 29) and the **Museo de Arte Popular**
  (Moneda 13). Two different altars, and both are set up for these dates.
- **Plaza de Santo Domingo.** The altar of the National Commission for the
  Disappeared.
- **The Centro, Academia and Moneda streets.** The Catrina parade.
- **The Zócalo floor.** The Day of the Dead installations.
- **Iztapalapa Cemetery.** The biggest pilgrimage: thousands of people on
  November 2.

## What to do

1. **Mixcoac at night**, on the 1st and 2nd. The candlelight is the show.
2. **San Fernando**, for the gravestones.
3. **The altars** at Casa de los Azulejos and the Museo de Arte Popular.
4. **The Santo Domingo altar**, which is not decorative: it is a denunciation.
5. **The sugar skulls** of La Merced, made by hand, one for each name.
6. **The Catrina parade**: a whole city dressed up.
7. **The masses for the dead** in the churches of the Centro, on the night of
   the 31st.
8. **The stands** of pan de muerto and pumpkin chocolate.
9. **A lit cemetery** in Xochimilco or Iztapalapa.
10. **Posada**: his Oaxacan calavera from 1903 is where all of this starts.

## What to eat

- **Pan de muerto.** Soft, sweet, sugar on top. Eaten in family, and nobody
  eats it alone.
- **Tamales of the dead and atole.** Hot, with cinnamon.
- **Pozole.** The same one as September 16: here it is eaten on the 1st and
  the 2nd.
- **Pumpkin in a mole**, and sweet **mushrooms** in cream.
- **Pumpkin chocolate** with the pan de muerto.

## Understanding Mexicans

Mexico does not celebrate death: it **visits** it. That is the difference
with Halloween, which is a joke about a stranger's death. Here it is the
grandfather, and you bring him what he liked.

- **The altar is an altar**, not a decoration: it has water, salt, bread,
  fruit, candles and the photo. It is addressed and it stays there all night.
- **The dead are still family.** They are not in the past: they are in the
  memory of the house, and the meal on the 1st includes them.
- **The death that is laughed at is ours.** The Catrina was born as "Calavera
  Garbancera" by Posada in 1910, to mock the European upper classes; Mexico
  turned her into the affectionate skeleton of the whole country.

And there is a grief that is not private: the Commission altar remembers the
tens of thousands who disappeared. On those dates, Mexico does not only
remember its dead: it complains about them.

## Why it matters at Calpulli CDMX

Because it is the clearest example of what we want to do with this archive:
explain what is happening, not only where to eat. The Day of the Dead is the
party where the city tells you who it is. It is **pinned**, and it will be
here in November, in February and in July, because it is not a seasonal tip:
it is part of how the city explains itself.

> **Note:** mass times, cemetery access and the activities change every year.
> Check with the cemeteries, the Museo de Arte Popular and the Mexico City
> government before you go.
